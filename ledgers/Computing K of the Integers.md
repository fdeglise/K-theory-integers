### Theo · 2026-10-28, 11:38:28 AM GMT-7

For **Quillen’s algebraic \(K\)-groups \(K_n(\mathbb Z)\)**, the main strategy is to compute the **rank** and the **torsion** separately. Four methods are especially useful.

**1. Borel’s theorem: compute the rational part in every degree.**

For \(n\geq2\),
\[
K_n(\mathbb Z)\otimes\mathbb Q\cong
\begin{cases}
\mathbb Q,&n\equiv1\pmod4,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Borel obtains this through the stable real cohomology of arithmetic groups. Thus \(K_5(\mathbb Z)\) has rank one, while \(K_6(\mathbb Z)\) is finite. Determining their torsion requires further work. [Borel’s paper, §12](https://www.numdam.org/item/10.24033/asens.1269.pdf)

**2. Symbols and localization: particularly explicit for \(K_2\).**

Matsumoto’s theorem presents \(K_2(\mathbb Q)\) using symbols \(\{a,b\}\), bilinearity, and the Steinberg relation \(\{a,1-a\}=0\). Localization identifies
\[
K_2(\mathbb Z)
=
\ker\!\left(
K_2(\mathbb Q)\xrightarrow{(\partial_p)_p}
\bigoplus_p\mathbb F_p^\times
\right),
\]
where \(\partial_p\) is the tame symbol. Computing this kernel gives
\[
K_2(\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z/2,
\]
generated by \(\{-1,-1\}\). This is a good first example of an explicit integral calculation. [Weibel, Chapter III](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~weibel/Kbook/Kbook.III.pdf)

In higher degrees, localization similarly relates \(K_*(\mathbb Z)\), \(K_*(\mathbb Q)\), and finite-field \(K\)-groups. Quillen computed the latter:
\[
K_{2r}(\mathbb F_q)=0,\qquad
K_{2r-1}(\mathbb F_q)\cong\mathbb Z/(q^r-1),
\quad r\geq1.
\]
The remaining input is information about \(K_*(\mathbb Q)\). [Quillen’s paper](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/1972/96-3/p07)

**3. Étale cohomology and arithmetic: compute one prime at a time.**

For an odd prime \(p\) and \(i\geq2\), the Quillen–Lichtenbaum comparison gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
K_{2i-2}(\mathbb Z)\otimes\mathbb Z_p
&\cong H^2_{\mathrm{\acute et}}
  (\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z[1/p],\mathbb Z_p(i)),\\
K_{2i-1}(\mathbb Z)\otimes\mathbb Z_p
&\cong H^1_{\mathrm{\acute et}}
  (\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z[1/p],\mathbb Z_p(i)).
\end{aligned}
\]
These groups can be studied through cyclotomic units, class groups, and Iwasawa theory. Bernoulli numbers determine important torsion orders; for example, the numerator \(691\) of \(B_{12}\) appears in
\[
K_{22}(\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z/691.
\]
[Weibel, Chapter VI, §§8–10](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~weibel/Kbook/Kbook.VI.pdf)

The prime \(2\) requires additional treatment because of the real embedding of \(\mathbb Q\). Motivic spectral sequences and their differentials determine the \(2\)-primary torsion, including the order-\(16\) parts of both \(K_3(\mathbb Z)\) and \(K_7(\mathbb Z)\). [Rognes–Weibel](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~weibel/archive/papers-dir/RognesWeibel.pdf)

**4. Arithmetic-group homology and Voronoi complexes: explicit computer-assisted calculations.**

Quillen’s construction gives
\[
K_n(\mathbb Z)=\pi_{n+1}(BQ\mathbb Z).
\]
The computational approach proceeds through:

- Voronoi decompositions of spaces of positive definite quadratic forms;
- integer boundary matrices and their Smith normal forms;
- homology of \(GL_N(\mathbb Z)\) with Steinberg-module coefficients;
- the rank filtration and Hurewicz maps connecting this homology to \(K\)-theory.

Controlling the Hurewicz kernels and small-prime torsion is essential. Combined with arithmetic results, this approach establishes
\[
K_5(\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z,\qquad K_6(\mathbb Z)=0.
\]
[Elbaz-Vincent–Gangl–Soulé](https://www.ihes.fr/~soule/documents/YAIMA4438.pdf)

A later computation using Voronoi complexes in ranks \(8\)–\(11\), together with arithmetic input, proves \(K_8(\mathbb Z)=0\). [Dutour Sikirić–Elbaz-Vincent–Kupers–Martinet, Theorem 6.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1910.11598v1)

For orientation, the resulting low-degree groups are:

| \(n\) | \(K_n(\mathbb Z)\) |
|---:|:---|
| 2 | \(\mathbb Z/2\) |
| 3 | \(\mathbb Z/48\) |
| 4 | \(0\) |
| 5 | \(\mathbb Z\) |
| 6 | \(0\) |
| 7 | \(\mathbb Z/240\) |
| 8 | \(0\) |
| 9 | \(\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/2\) |
| 10 | \(\mathbb Z/2\) |
| 11 | \(\mathbb Z/1008\) |

See [Weibel’s table](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~weibel/Kbook/Kbook.VI.pdf), with its formerly unresolved \(K_8\) entry settled by the paper above.

### Theo · 2026-10-28, 11:58:09 AM GMT-7

For \(K_8(\mathbb Z)\), there is a complete proof that
\[
\boxed{K_8(\mathbb Z)=0.}
\]
Its computational part uses quadratic forms to calculate arithmetic-group homology. Two further ingredients—control of the Hurewicz map and arithmetic information at small primes—turn that calculation into a statement about \(K\)-theory.

The same framework gives precise targets for \(K_{12}\) and \(K_{16}\), whose vanishing remains conjectural. I will distinguish the established computation from the additional work those extensions require.

**1. The first reduction: replace \(K\)-theory by arithmetic-group homology.**

Quillen’s construction gives a space \(BQ\) with
\[
K_m(\mathbb Z)=\pi_{m+1}(BQ).
\]
Filtering it by the rank of free abelian groups produces a spectral sequence
\[
E^1_{N,q}
=
H_q\!\left(\mathrm{GL}_N(\mathbb Z);\mathrm{St}_N\right)
\Longrightarrow H_{N+q}(BQ;\mathbb Z).
\]
Here \(\mathrm{St}_N\) is the Steinberg module: the top reduced homology of the Tits building of proper nonzero subspaces of \(\mathbb Q^N\).

Thus the homology relevant to \(K_m\) lies on the diagonal
\[
N+q=m+1.
\]
This is the bridge from \(K\)-theory to a calculation involving finitely many ranks. [Kupers, *A short proof that \(K_8(\mathbb Z)\cong0\)*, §2](https://www.utsc.utoronto.ca/people/kupers/wp-content/uploads/sites/50/2021/01/k8zshorter.pdf).

**2. Quadratic forms give a finite complex for these groups.**

For a positive definite quadratic form \(Q\), let
\[
M(Q)=\{v\in\mathbb Z^N\setminus\{0\}:Q[v]=\min Q\}.
\]
Its minimal vectors generate a polyhedral cone
\[
C(Q)=\operatorname{cone}\{vv^{\mathsf T}:v\in M(Q)\}.
\]
Perfect forms determine the maximal cones of the Voronoi decomposition; their faces supply the other cells. Quotienting by positive scaling gives projective cells.

After inverting primes at most \(N+1\), the associated Voronoi chain complex satisfies
\[
H_j(\mathrm{Vor}_N;R)
\cong
H_{j-N+1}\!\left(\mathrm{GL}_N(\mathbb Z);
\mathrm{St}_N\otimes R\right).
\]
Combining the two degree shifts gives a useful rule:
\[
\boxed{\text{For }K_m,\text{ the relevant Voronoi homology is }H_m(\mathrm{Vor}_N).}
\]
I use **projective cell dimension** here; a corresponding cone has dimension one greater. [Dutour Sikirić–Elbaz-Vincent–Kupers–Martinet, §§1 and 6](https://jamartin.perso.math.cnrs.fr/Publications/DEKM.pdf).

The localization accounts for finite cell stabilizers. It also simplifies orientations: if a stabilizer reverses a cell’s orientation, that cell contributes zero after inverting \(2\). [Elbaz-Vincent–Gangl–Soulé, §§2–3](https://www.ihes.fr/~soule/documents/YAIMA4438.pdf).

**3. Enumerate the cells, with a proof that the enumeration is exhaustive.**

There are two complementary algorithms.

The general Voronoi algorithm works with the Ryshkov polyhedron
\[
\mathcal R_N=
\{Q>0:Q[v]\ge1\text{ for every }0\ne v\in\mathbb Z^N\}.
\]
Its vertices are normalized perfect forms. The algorithm is:

1. Start with a known perfect form.
2. Compute its minimal vectors and the edges leaving its vertex.
3. Follow each edge to the neighboring perfect form, using exact short-vector searches.
4. Identify equivalent forms under \(\mathrm{GL}_N(\mathbb Z)\).
5. Continue until every neighbor belongs to an already recorded orbit.
6. Enumerate the required faces of these perfect cones.

The graph is connected and has finitely many vertex orbits, so this is a terminating algorithm in each fixed rank. Its difficulty is the size of the enumeration. [Schürmann, *Enumerating perfect forms*, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.1587).

For the \(K_8\) computation, a more economical approach enumerates small-dimensional cells directly. Choose \(N\) independent minimal vectors, generating a sublattice \(L'\subset L\). If \(\gamma_N\) is the Hermite constant, Hadamard’s inequality gives
\[
[L:L']\le \gamma_N^{N/2}.
\]
An explicit upper bound for \(\gamma_N\) therefore bounds the possible indices. One enumerates the resulting quotient lattices, represented by finite codes or integer normal forms, and tests possible minimal-vector configurations.

The realization test asks whether a symmetric matrix \(A>0\) can satisfy
\[
A[v]=1\quad(v\in V),\qquad
A[w]>1\quad(w\notin\pm V).
\]
These are linear conditions in the entries of \(A\), apart from positive definiteness and the infinite family of inequalities. Short-vector enumeration supplies violated inequalities successively. Exact rational calculations certify feasibility or infeasibility; proven bounds are needed to certify completeness. [Keller–Martinet–Schürmann, *On classifying Minkowskian sublattices*, §4](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.3110).

The scale explains the importance of targeting small cells: the recent complete rank-\(9\) classification contains over **two billion** perfect lattices, before computing all their faces. [Dutour Sikirić–van Woerden, *The lattice packing problem in dimension 9 by Voronoi’s algorithm*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20719).

**4. Construct boundary matrices and compute integral homology.**

For \(H_m(\mathrm{Vor}_N)\), the required chain segment is
\[
V_{m+1}\xrightarrow{d_{m+1}}V_m
\xrightarrow{d_m}V_{m-1}.
\]

For each cell, compute its facets, identify their orbit representatives, and record signed incidence multiplicities, including orientation transport. These give sparse integer matrices.

Then compute
\[
H_m=\ker d_m/\operatorname{im}d_{m+1}
\]
by exact integer linear algebra:

- Put \(d_m\) into Smith normal form and extract an integral basis for its kernel.
- Express \(d_{m+1}\) in that kernel basis.
- Compute another Smith normal form.

The second step is essential: separately knowing the ranks or Smith forms of the two original matrices does not determine their homology. The computation also checks \(d_md_{m+1}=0\), orbit identifications, stabilizers, and enumeration completeness. These methods underlie the earlier rank-\(\le7\) calculations. [Elbaz-Vincent–Gangl–Soulé, §§3–4](https://www.ihes.fr/~soule/documents/YAIMA4438.pdf).

Working over \(\mathbb Q\) would discard the torsion we are trying to detect. Exact Smith invariants can exclude torsion at **all** remaining primes simultaneously.

**5. How these steps prove \(K_8(\mathbb Z)=0\).**

Set
\[
R=\mathbb Z[1/7!].
\]
On the diagonal \(N+q=9\), the required entries vanish:

| Rank \(N\) | Required group | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| \(1\le N\le7\) | \(H_{9-N}(\mathrm{GL}_N;\mathrm{St}_N)\otimes R\) | Earlier computations |
| \(N=8\) | \(H_1(\mathrm{GL}_8;\mathrm{St}_8)\otimes R\) | Voronoi computation, or a general vanishing theorem |
| \(N=9\) | \(H_0(\mathrm{GL}_9;\mathrm{St}_9)\otimes R\) | Lee–Szczarba’s theorem |

Consequently,
\[
H_9(BQ;R)=0.
\]
Because the entire diagonal is zero, spectral-sequence differentials cause no ambiguity. Kupers obtains this conclusion without new enumeration by using the Church–Putman vanishing theorem for the \(N=8\) entry. [Kupers, Lemmas 2.1–2.5](https://www.utsc.utoronto.ca/people/kupers/wp-content/uploads/sites/50/2021/01/k8zshorter.pdf).

Now use the Hurewicz map
\[
K_8(\mathbb Z)=\pi_9(BQ)\longrightarrow H_9(BQ;\mathbb Z).
\]
For a connected infinite loop space, its degree-\(n\) kernel has torsion only at primes at most \(n/2+1\). In degree \(9\), these primes are \(2,3,5\). Therefore the map becomes injective over \(R\), and
\[
K_8(\mathbb Z)\otimes R=0.
\]
[Dutour Sikirić–Elbaz-Vincent–Kupers–Martinet, Proposition 6.3](https://jamartin.perso.math.cnrs.fr/Publications/DEKM.pdf).

Finally, arithmetic removes the discarded primes. The groups \(K_{4r}(\mathbb Z)\) have no \(2\)-primary torsion, and positive even \(K\)-groups have no torsion at an odd regular prime. Since \(3,5,7\) are regular,
\[
K_8(\mathbb Z)=0.
\]
[Weibel, *The K-book*, VI, Corollary 9.8 and Proposition 10.5](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~weibel/Kbook/Kbook.VI.pdf).

**6. The precise extension toward \(K_{12}\).**

The relevant diagonal is
\[
N+q=13,
\]
and the Voronoi target remains a single homological degree:
\[
H_{12}(\mathrm{Vor}_N).
\]

The authors explicitly identify the following missing calculations for their proposed extension:

| Rank | Voronoi homology | Equivalent Steinberg homology |
|---|---|---|
| \(9\) | \(H_{12}(\mathrm{Vor}_9)\) | \(H_4(\mathrm{GL}_9;\mathrm{St}_9)\) |
| \(10\) | \(H_{12}(\mathrm{Vor}_{10})\) | \(H_3(\mathrm{GL}_{10};\mathrm{St}_{10})\) |
| \(11\) | \(H_{12}(\mathrm{Vor}_{11})\) | \(H_2(\mathrm{GL}_{11};\mathrm{St}_{11})\) |

Each requires the chain segment \(V_{13}\to V_{12}\to V_{11}\), or a theorem replacing that calculation. [Dutour Sikirić–Elbaz-Vincent–Kupers–Martinet, Remark 6.6](https://jamartin.perso.math.cnrs.fr/Publications/DEKM.pdf).

There is an additional feature: the appropriate goal is **torsion-free homology**, rather than zero homology.

Combining Borel’s ranks with the rational structure of an infinite loop space gives
\[
H_*(BQ;\mathbb Q)
\cong
\Lambda(a_1)\otimes\mathbb Q[b_6,b_{10},b_{14},\ldots].
\]
Thus, by a degree calculation,
\[
H_{13}(BQ;\mathbb Q)=\mathbb Q\{a_1b_6^2\}.
\]
This class is a product of lower-degree classes; it does not represent a nonzero rational \(K_{12}\). [Borel, *Stable real cohomology of arithmetic groups*, §12](https://www.numdam.org/item/10.24033/asens.1269.pdf).

For example, take \(R_{12}=\mathbb Z[1/13!]\). A sufficient computational result would be
\[
H_{13}(BQ;R_{12})\cong R_{12}.
\]
Indeed, \(K_{12}\) is finite, and localized Hurewicz injectivity would embed it into a torsion-free module. Arithmetic then removes the inverted primes.

Unlike the \(K_8\) case, nonzero spectral-sequence entries require control of differentials and extensions. Computing the listed groups is part of that argument, rather than permission to ignore those maps.

**7. What changes for \(K_{16}\).**

Now
\[
N+q=17,\qquad j=16.
\]
After applying the general \(H_0\)- and \(H_1\)-vanishing results and using the earlier rank-\(\le7\) calculations, the natural additional range is
\[
H_{16}(\mathrm{Vor}_N),\qquad 8\le N\le15.
\]
The corresponding chain segments are
\[
V_{17}\longrightarrow V_{16}\longrightarrow V_{15}.
\]
This range follows directly from the degree formulas; it is a computational target, not an assertion that every group in it vanishes.

The same rational calculation gives
\[
H_{17}(BQ;\mathbb Q)
=\mathbb Q\{a_1b_6b_{10}\}.
\]
Thus a sufficient result, with \(R_{16}=\mathbb Z[1/17!]\), would be
\[
H_{17}(BQ;R_{16})\cong R_{16}.
\]
Hurewicz injectivity and the arithmetic small-prime results would then imply \(K_{16}(\mathbb Z)=0\).

A promising way to reduce these enumerations is to construct partial resolutions of Steinberg modules and prove vanishing near the virtual cohomological dimension
\[
\nu_N=\frac{N(N-1)}2.
\]
Borel–Serre duality relates this to the required Steinberg homology, with an orientation twist for \(\mathrm{GL}_N\). Recent work proves rational codimension-two vanishing using such resolutions. The needed extension must retain control of integral torsion after localization; the rational theorem alone does not supply that certificate. [Brück–Miller–Patzt–Sroka–Wilson, *On the codimension-two cohomology of \(\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb Z)\)*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.11967).

**A useful research program can be coded, but there is no established method that I can promise will compute \(K_{16}(\mathbb Z)\) in a reasonable time.** An unconditional determination would be a new mathematical result; the expected answer is \(0\). [Weibel, *The K-book*, VI.10](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~weibel/Kbook/Kbook.VI.pdf).

I should qualify my previous explanation: it described a proof strategy. The Voronoi algorithms compute localized homology; identifying \(K_{16}\) also requires spectral-sequence and homotopy arguments.

The main obstacles are these:

- **Constructing the input.** Exact linear algebra needs an exhaustive list of cells and their incidences. Even rank \(9\) has \(2{,}237{,}251{,}040\) perfect lattices. This illustrates the cost of full enumeration, although targeted enumeration of lower-dimensional cells can be much smaller. [Dutour Sikirić–van Woerden](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20719).
- **Certifying torsion.** Rational calculations determine ranks but discard the possible finite group we want to detect. Computations modulo several primes also need an additional argument to exclude every other prime.
- **Completing the comparison with \(K\)-theory.** Knowing the groups on the first page of a spectral sequence generally leaves differentials and extensions to resolve. The \(K_8\) proof benefits from a completely vanishing diagonal. [Dutour Sikirić–Elbaz-Vincent–Kupers–Martinet, §6](https://jamartin.perso.math.cnrs.fr/Publications/DEKM.pdf).

There is nevertheless a concrete computational objective. Put
\[
R=\mathbb Z[1/17!].
\]
A certificate that
\[
\boxed{H_{17}(BQ;R)\cong R}
\]
would suffice to prove \(K_{16}(\mathbb Z)=0\): the finite group \(K_{16}\otimes R\) injects into this torsion-free homology group, and known arithmetic results exclude torsion at the inverted primes. This is a sufficient criterion derived from the comparison arguments—not a computation already available.

A sensible implementation would therefore have the following components:

| Component | Feasibility |
|---|---|
| Exact lattice equivalence, stabilizers, orientations | Established algorithms |
| Boundary matrices and exact homology | Established algorithms; cost depends on matrix size |
| Exhaustive enumeration in the required degrees and ranks | Main computational uncertainty |
| Spectral-sequence and \(K\)-theory certificate | Requires mathematical arguments beyond matrix reduction |

The strategy with the best prospect is to **reduce the required enumeration mathematically**, then compute only the surviving chain segments. Partial resolutions of Steinberg modules are relevant here; recent codimension-two results demonstrate this approach, although their rational vanishing theorem alone does not eliminate integral torsion. [Brück–Miller–Patzt–Sroka–Wilson](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.11967).

For a first implementation, I would reproduce known arithmetic-group homology calculations, then tackle one of the missing \(K_{12}\) targets:
\[
H_{12}(\mathrm{Vor}_N),\qquad N=9,10,11.
\]
These are explicitly identified in the \(K_8\) paper as the remaining computations for its proposed \(K_{12}\) extension. [Remark 6.6](https://jamartin.perso.math.cnrs.fr/Publications/DEKM.pdf).

That pilot would measure cell counts, matrix sizes, and exact-arithmetic costs. Those measurements are needed before giving a defensible runtime estimate for a \(K_{16}\) computation.
